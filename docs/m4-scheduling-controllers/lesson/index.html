Scheduling & Controllers — How Intent Becomes Reality

M1 left you with a Pod object sitting in etcd with no node assigned. It was paperwork, not a running container. M2 and M3 followed that write down to durable storage and back. This module is about the two specialists that turn that paperwork into a running workload, and what you read when they cannot. On call, this is the most common failure pattern you will meet: a Pod stuck in Pending, or a node that flaps and takes your Pods with it. Both are the work of the scheduler and the controllers you will meet here. And both, done right, tell you the reason out loud. Your whole job in those moments is to read the reason before you touch anything.

The scheduler as a pipeline: a hostel room allocation​

Think about how a hostel or a PG allocates a room to a new student. The warden does not look at the best room first. He starts with a simpler question: which rooms even fit this student? A ground-floor room, because the student cannot climb stairs. Non-smoking. A spare bed actually free. Any room that fails even one of these is out. It does not matter how nice it is. Only after that shortlist does he rank what is left: this one gets more morning light, that one is closer to the mess. Then he picks the best of the ones that fit, and hands over the key. Two steps, always in that order. First which rooms qualify, then which of those is best.

The Kubernetes scheduler runs the same two steps, with a few more around them. It is a pipeline, and a Pod walks through it once per scheduling attempt:

  • Queue. Every unscheduled Pod waits here. This is the informer-fed backlog the scheduler works through.
  • Filter. The warden's first question. Each node is checked against the Pod's hard requirements: does it have enough CPU and memory left, does it match the nodeSelector, is it free of a taint this Pod cannot tolerate. A node that fails any filter is dropped. If every node is dropped, the Pod stays Pending, and the scheduler records why.
  • Score. The ranking. Every surviving node gets points for things like spare capacity and spreading Pods evenly. The highest score wins.
  • Reserve → permit → bind. The scheduler holds the winning node so two Pods do not grab the same room, runs any final gate, then writes the binding. As you saw in M1, that binding is just another API write. The kubelet on that node watches for it and starts the containers.

Filter is a yes/no gate. Score is a ranking of whatever passed. Keep those two separate in your head and most scheduling behaviour will make sense.

Taints and tolerations: the reserved section​

There is one filter worth its own picture. A taint on a node is like a reserved section in a wedding hall, the row kept for the bride's family. You cannot sit there just because a chair is empty. You need a pass. In Kubernetes, that pass is a toleration on the Pod. A node with a taint repels every Pod that does not carry the matching toleration, even if that node has plenty of room. This is not the node being full. It is the node saying you are not allowed here. Control-plane nodes are tainted this way so your ordinary workloads never land on them, and you will see the same mechanism again in a moment doing something much more important: protecting you from a failing node.

Pending Pod Events Identify Scheduling Failures​

Here is the habit this module builds, and it is the same reading habit M1 gave you for status and M2 gave you for the audit log. When a Pod is Pending, do not guess. Run kubectl describe pod, go to the Events, and read the FailedScheduling message. The scheduler writes down exactly why it could not place the Pod. There are three distinct causes, and they need three different fixes:

  1. The request is too big for any node. The Pod asks for more CPU or memory than any single node has free. The reason reads like Insufficient cpu or Insufficient memory. The fix is on the workload or the cluster: smaller requests, or a bigger node. No amount of waiting will help.
  2. The selector matches nothing. The Pod has a nodeSelector or affinity rule, and no node carries the label it asks for. The reason names the unmatched predicate, node(s) didn't match node selector. Often the label is simply mistyped, or the node pool you targeted does not exist.
  3. A taint it cannot tolerate. Every candidate node carries a taint the Pod has no toleration for. The reason reads node(s) had untolerated taint. The room exists and is empty; the Pod just does not have the pass.

Three causes, three reasons, three fixes, and the scheduler names which one you are looking at. A learner who guesses will scale the cluster to fix a mistyped label and change nothing. A learner who reads the reason first fixes it in one move. This is the whole point of the lab: you will seed all three and read each one straight off the Pod.

Read the reason before you act

Pending is not a single condition. It is a family, and describe tells you which member you have. Insufficient cpu, didn't match node selector, and untolerated taint send you to three different places: the workload, the labels, the taints. This is the M1 reading habit applied one layer up: the system already wrote down the answer, so read it before you change anything.

Kubernetes Controllers Share a Reconciliation Pattern​

The scheduler is one specialist. Behind it sits a whole family of controllers, and once you have seen how one is built you have seen them all. Every controller is the M1 reconcile loop (observe, diff, act) wrapped in the same standard machinery:

  • An informer watches the API server and keeps a local cache, so the controller reads from memory, not from etcd, the same watch-cache economy you saw in M2.
  • Changes land on a work queue that de-duplicates. Ten updates to the same object while the controller is busy collapse into one item of work.
  • Rate limiting and backoff keep a failing reconcile from hammering the API server. A Pod that will not schedule is retried after a short wait, then a longer one, so one broken object does not become a busy loop.
  • Leader election decides who runs. In an HA control plane several copies of a controller are up, but only one holds the Lease (the same heartbeat you met in the M1 deep dive) and does the work. The others wait to take over if the leader's heartbeat stops.

That is the basic pattern of every controller in the cluster. The node-controller is the one worth studying, because it is where this machinery meets the taints from earlier and decides the fate of your Pods.

Node Conditions Trigger Taints and Pod Eviction​

The node-controller watches one thing: the health of every node. Each kubelet reports conditions (Ready, MemoryPressure, DiskPressure) and renews a Lease every few seconds to say I am still here. The node-controller reads these the way the reconcile loop reads any observed state. When a node stops renewing its Lease, or reports Ready as false, the controller acts, and it acts through the exact taint mechanism from the reserved-section analogy:

When a node goes NotReady, the node-controller taints it: node.kubernetes.io/not-ready or node.kubernetes.io/unreachable. That taint does two jobs at once. First, the scheduler now filters the node out, so no new Pod lands on a node that may be dying. Second, the Pods already running there get an eviction timer. Every Pod carries a quiet default toleration for these taints with a tolerationSeconds (usually 300) which is really a grace period: tolerate an unreachable node for five minutes, then give up. If the node comes back before the timer expires, the taint clears and nothing moves. If it does not, the Pods are deleted and rescheduled onto healthy nodes.

This is why a node that flaps (goes NotReady and recovers, again and again) is such a nasty on-call event. Each flap can start eviction timers; a slow flap past the grace period evicts and reschedules Pods, and the churn lands on the scheduler and the API server together. And it is why tolerationSeconds is a real tuning knob: too short and a brief network blip evicts healthy Pods; too long and genuinely dead nodes hold their workloads hostage. In the lab you will flip a fake node to NotReady with KWOK and watch this whole chain fire (the taint appear, the timer start, the Pods move) on your own cluster.

Scheduling Policy Defines Placement Before Failure​

The scheduler can only apply the policy you gave it. If every team invents requests, affinities, tolerations, and priorities independently, a capacity incident becomes a policy incident as well.

Resource requestsSet requests from measured usage plus a justified buffer. Review them after workload or traffic changes.Missing requests, or requests copied from another service. Both make placement and autoscaling misleading.FailedScheduling events, requested versus allocatable resources, and recent usage.

Test it together with replica count and zone spread.Shortening the timer without checking whether the replacement has somewhere healthy to run.Lease age, node condition transition, taint timestamp, toleration expiry, and replacement readiness.

When a Pod is Pending, use this order: read the event, identify the scheduler plugin or constraint, confirm the live node inventory, and only then decide whether to change the workload or add capacity. Recreating the Pod, removing requests, adding a universal toleration, or assigning a higher priority may hide the symptom while making the cluster less predictable.

Lab: Diagnose and Resolve Pending Pods​

In the lab you will seed three Pending Pods, one for each cause, and read each FailedScheduling reason straight off the Pod before you fix it. Then you will bring up KWOK inside your cluster, add fake nodes, and flip one to NotReady. And watch the node-controller taint it, the scheduler avoid it, and the eviction timers move the Pods. The scheduler and controllers stop being a black box the moment you can read what they are telling you. That reading habit is what the rest of the course runs on.
