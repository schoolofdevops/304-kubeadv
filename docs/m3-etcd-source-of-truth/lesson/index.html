etcd — The Cluster's Source of Truth

M2 followed a write through the checkpoints until "etcd write" made it durable, and left it there. This module opens that box. Every object in your cluster (every Pod, Secret, lease, RBAC binding) is a key in etcd; everything else you've studied so far is a cache rebuilt from it: the informer stores of M1, the watch cache of M2. That makes etcd the one component whose failures you cannot route around. When etcd is slow, every kubectl command and every controller in the cluster is slow. When etcd is full, every write in the cluster fails at once: deploys, scale-ups, even the lease renewals that keep nodes looking alive. In this module's lab you will drive your own course cluster into exactly that failure and bring it back.

etcd Quorum Requires a Majority​

Picture a bank vault that opens only when a majority of its keyholders turn their keys together. With three keyholders, any two suffice. One can be home sick and business continues. But if two are unreachable, the vault stays shut for everyone, including customers with entirely legitimate requests. The vault isn't damaged; the rule that protects its contents is doing its job.

etcd replicates its data with the Raft consensus protocol, and Raft is that vault. A write is committed only when a quorum has persisted it to the write-ahead log. Quorum means a strict majority: floor(n/2)+1, where n is the number of voting members. Three members need 2 votes; five need 3. You may also see (n/2)+1 in code or diagrams where / means integer division, but writing floor(n/2)+1 makes the rounding explicit.

Even member counts buy nothing: four members still tolerate only one failure but add a fourth machine that participates in replication. That is why production etcd clusters normally use three or five voting members.

One member is elected leader; all writes flow through it, and it confirms its authority with heartbeats to the followers. A follower that stops hearing heartbeats past its election timeout calls a new election. This machinery matters operationally because of what lost quorum means: with a majority of members down, no leader can be elected, so etcd accepts no writes and no linearizable reads. Your workloads keep running (the data-plane inertia you saw in M1), but the cluster is unmanageable: nothing deploys, nothing scales, no failed Pod is replaced. It is an availability loss, not a data loss: restore one member, quorum returns, and the log replays.

Disk Latency Can Disrupt Raft Leadership​

Think of the leader's heartbeat as a pacemaker. It must keep a steady rhythm so followers know the leader is alive. The default heartbeat interval is 100 ms. If a follower hears nothing for the election timeout—1000 ms by default—it stops trusting the old leader and starts an election.

Now connect that clock to the disk, as the slide does. etcd cannot call a proposal durable merely because it reached memory:

  1. A write reaches the leader.
  2. The leader appends the proposal to its Raft write-ahead log (WAL) and waits for fsync.
  3. Followers persist the proposal and acknowledge it.
  4. Once a quorum has persisted the proposal, it can be committed.
  5. At the same time, the leader must keep serving Raft heartbeats and peer traffic.

A one-second storage stall is therefore not "only a slow write." On the default timings, it is long enough to collide with the election timeout. Disk contention, CPU starvation, or network delay can prevent Raft from making progress. Followers may start an election even though the old leader process is still running. During the leadership gap, cluster-wide writes pause, so the API-server latency spike appears in deploys, status updates, leases, and controller work at the same time.

What the symptom means in production​

Repeated leader changes while every member remains up are a strong clue that the cluster is suffering a latency or resource problem. The disk is the first place to investigate, but do not stop at the clue and declare the cause. CPU starvation and peer-network delay can create a similar failure pattern.

Read the evidence together:

What I recommend​

  • Give each etcd member fast, predictable, dedicated storage. Do not share the volume with container images, application logs, or another write-heavy service.
  • Keep members in nearby failure zones with low and predictable peer latency. A quorum spread across distant regions turns network variance into control-plane instability.
  • Alert on WAL fsync p99, backend commit latency, and leader changes before users report slow kubectl commands.
  • Do not increase the election timeout merely to hide a slow disk. Fix storage, CPU, or network pressure first. Tune heartbeat and election values only when measured peer RTT requires it, and keep the values consistent across members.
  • During an incident, preserve the timeline: leader change, WAL fsync spike, backend commit spike, member restart, CPU throttling, and peer-network errors. One chart alone is not a diagnosis.
The practical reading of the slide

The pocket watch represents the Raft timing contract. The hard disk represents durable progress. When the leader keeps changing but no member is down, check WAL fsync p99 first—then confirm the diagnosis against CPU, network, and restart evidence.

MVCC Preserves Revision History​

A shopkeeper's bahi-khata (the bound ledger that sits by the till) has one strict rule: you never erase an entry. A correction is a new entry that supersedes the old one, and even closing an account is itself written into the ledger. The history is the point. It is what lets an auditor reconstruct the books as they stood on any past date.

etcd's storage engine works exactly this way. It is MVCC (multi-version concurrency control), built around a single, global, monotonically increasing revision counter. Every write to any key creates a new revision. Updating a key does not overwrite the old value; it appends a new version of that key at the new revision. Deleting a key appends a tombstone: an entry that says "this key ended here." History accumulates with every write, by design.

That design is what makes the watch machinery of M1 and M2 possible: a watcher can say "give me every change since revision N" and etcd replays it from history, no polling, no diffing. Here is the bridge to what you already know: for resources stored by kube-apiserver in etcd, the numeric resourceVersion you see is derived from the storage revision. That is why the values in this lab line up with etcd revisions. The Kubernetes client contract is still stricter: treat resourceVersion as a server-owned token and pass it back unchanged. In Kubernetes 1.35 you may order numeric resource versions only when both objects come from the same API resource type. Do not compare a Pod's value with a Deployment's value to build a cluster-wide timeline.

resourceVersion, decoded

Treat resourceVersion as an opaque token in code. In this kube-apiserver and etcd path, it reflects the storage revision at which the object changed. That implementation detail helps you understand watch replay, but it is not permission to do arithmetic on the value or compare unrelated resource types. Use audit records when you need a cross-resource incident timeline.

Compaction and Defragmentation Reclaim Different Space​

This is the pair of operations most operators conflate, and confusing them during an incident means your recovery does nothing. Stay in the ledger: compaction is shredding every page older than a chosen date. You can no longer audit history before that point, but the binder itself is just as thick; the freed pages are simply available to be written over. Defragmentation is copying the surviving entries into a fresh, thin binder and returning the old one's shelf space.

Concretely: etcdctl compaction <rev> drops all key history before revision <rev>. It frees logical space (the database's in-use bytes drop, and the freed pages are reused for future writes), but the file on disk does not shrink by a single byte. Its operational side effect: any watcher trying to resume from a revision older than the compaction point gets required revision has been compacted and must re-list: M2's resync-storm mechanics, triggered from the storage layer. In a kubeadm cluster you rarely run compaction by hand, because the kube-apiserver requests one every five minutes (--etcd-compaction-interval). Nobody, however, defragments for you.

etcdctl defrag rewrites the database file, dropping the free pages, and is the only operation that returns disk to the operating system. It blocks the member while it runs, so in a multi-member cluster you defragment one member at a time, leader last.

Order matters and is worth saying plainly: defragmenting before compacting does nothing, because every page still holds live history, there is nothing free to drop. Compact first, then defragment. You will prove this sequence on your own cluster in the lab.

Backend Quota Controls etcd Database Growth​

Because history accumulates with every write, an etcd database grows without bound unless something stops it. The backend quota (--quota-backend-bytes, 2 GB by default, 8 GB the practical ceiling) is that stop: a circuit breaker that trips before etcd can eat the disk out from under its own WAL and the node.

The lifecycle is precise, and you will drive every stage of it in the lab. When the database file crosses the quota, the member raises a NOSPACE alarm. The alarm propagates cluster-wide, and etcd goes read-only: every write is rejected with etcdserver: mvcc: database space exceeded, while reads and watches keep working. Now translate "every write" into Kubernetes terms: not just your deploys: kubelet node-lease renewals, controller leader-election renewals, status updates. A full etcd does not present as "creates are failing"; it presents as the entire control plane failing at once, while kubectl get innocently keeps answering.

Recovery is the sequence this lesson has been building toward: compact (free logical space), defragment (shrink the file below quota), then etcdctl alarm disarm. The alarm never clears itself. That is deliberate. Disarming without actually freeing space would just re-trip on the next write, so etcd insists a human confirms the space is really back before it resumes writing.

Snapshots Define the etcd Recovery Point​

etcdctl snapshot save produces a point-in-time copy of the entire keyspace in one file, bounded, conveniently, by the same quota that bounds the database. etcdutl snapshot status inspects one; restoring builds a new cluster from it, with new member identities, and every API server cache, informer, and controller re-syncs to the restored truth. Anything written after the snapshot is gone, which is why restore is a last resort and why an untested snapshot is not a backup. It is a hope. The instructor extension for this module walks the full destructive restore; the deep dive shows you snapshot inspection hands-on.

OpenAI Isolates Kubernetes Events in a Separate etcd Cluster​

Kubernetes Events are the noisiest tenant in the keyspace: high-volume, constantly written, TTL'd after an hour, and individually almost worthless. In a shared etcd they spend revisions, inflate history between compactions, and consume database size and watch bandwidth alongside the objects your cluster cannot live without. When OpenAI scaled the 7,500-node cluster you met in M2, they moved Events into a dedicated etcd cluster using the API server's --etcd-servers-overrides flag: a per-resource storage override, invisible to every client. The transferable lesson sharpens this module's theme: the quota protects the database from unbounded growth, but separation protects your source of truth from its own noisiest data. Same instinct as M2's FlowSchema isolation, applied one layer down.

Production etcd Requires Explicit Operating Limits​

In production, etcd is not a database that you tune after it becomes slow. You decide its failure boundary, storage, maintenance, and recovery process before the cluster carries business workloads.

The production rule

Do not solve an etcd incident by changing several controls together. First classify the failure: quorum, disk latency, quota, corruption, or client pressure. Preserve a snapshot before destructive recovery. Then make the smallest change that addresses that failure and verify writes, watches, leases, and controller progress.

Lab: Trigger and Recover from an etcd NOSPACE Alarm​

In the lab you'll verify your cluster carries the course's deliberately small 256 MB quota, set up etcdctl against the etcd static pod, baseline the database, then fill it with bounded junk writes until the NOSPACE alarm trips. You'll prove the read-only failure pattern (writes rejected, reads fine), and then recover your own cluster in the right order: compact, defragment, disarm, verify. The cluster you fix is the one you keep: M4 continues on it.
